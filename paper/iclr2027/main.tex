\begin{abstract}
Confidence scores from single-cell perturbation predictors are often used to
rank experiments, although the identity of easy and difficult perturbations
may change after a biological shift. We ask when such a reliability reordering
is identifiable, when it changes a decision, and what can be known before a new
experiment. In a source-frozen Frangieh ladder, one predictor is evaluated
unchanged across control, co-culture and IFN$\gamma$ outcomes using a tie-aware
pairwise ordering estimand and matched raw-cell measurement floors. The
measurement-identifiable excess is metric-dependent, while the conservative
joint-floor excess can be negative. At a fixed 10\% decision budget, the
prespecified full-depth link between identifiable excess and normalized regret
contains 18 directed transfer--metric points and is descriptive (Spearman
$\rho=0.80$). An outcome-blind Nadig HepG2/Jurkat replication and a source-only
held-out analysis delimit the result: context can reshape reliability ordering,
but measurement depth and prospective feature availability determine what can
be known before experiment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Single-cell perturbation models turn large screens into predictions that can
prioritize experiments over a combinatorial intervention space. Their value
therefore depends not only on average fidelity, but also on whether a researcher
can decide which predictions to trust before spending experimental budget. An
uncertainty score learned in one context need not preserve the same meaning in
another context. This question is related to, but distinct from, response
prediction itself.

Accuracy alone does not settle the decision problem. Deep perturbation
predictors do not yet reliably outperform simple linear baselines, while
Systema and scPertEval show that reference construction, split design and
metric choice can change conclusions~\citep{ahlmann2025simple,wong2025simple,systema2025,scperteval2026}.
The ordering of trusted versus risky predictions is therefore an object in its
own right.

Calibration and selective prediction ask whether a score tracks error in a
fixed task, while shift studies ask whether representations transport
\citep{guo2017calibration,geifman2017selective,ovadia2019uncertainty,liang2024selective}.
Neither alone establishes that the identity of easy and hard perturbations
survives a biological change~\citep{jalaldoust2024transport}. We study this
narrow operational question with source-frozen predictions and target-outcome-
blind selection.

Our central claim is an identifiability boundary: a visible cross-context
ordering difference becomes a scientific transport result only after the same
ordering statistic is compared with matched experimental and model floors.
Figures~\ref{fig:framework}--\ref{fig:prospective} summarize the path from
biological shift to ordering, measurement, decision consequence, heterogeneous
failure anatomy and the prospective information boundary.

\section{Reliability transport is an identifiable ordering problem}
For a perturbation pair $c=(p,q)$, context $e$, and measurement replicate $r$,
let $R_{p,e}^{(r)}$ be continuous risk from a fixed predictor and define
\begin{equation}
 Z_{c,e}^{(r)}=\operatorname{sign}\!\left(R_{p,e}^{(r)}-R_{q,e}^{(r)}\right)\in\{-1,0,+1\}.
\end{equation}
Ties are retained explicitly through
$\pi_{c,e}=[\Pr(Z=-1),\Pr(Z=0),\Pr(Z=+1)]$. For two contexts,
\begin{equation}
 D_{\rm cross}=\mathbb{E}_{c}[1-\pi_{c,e_1}^{\mathsf T}\pi_{c,e_2}],\qquad
 D_{\rm within,e}=\mathbb{E}_{c}[1-\|\pi_{c,e}\|_2^2].
\end{equation}
The population measurement-identifiable component is
\begin{equation}
 D_{\rm meas\text{-}ID}=D_{\rm cross}-
 \frac{D_{\rm within,e_1}+D_{\rm within,e_2}}{2}
 =\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{c}\|\pi_{c,e_1}-\pi_{c,e_2}\|_2^2\geq0.
 \label{eq:ordering_identity}
\end{equation}
This equality is an identity, not a causal statement. For finite replicates we
use the leave-one-replicate-out U-statistic
\begin{equation}
 \widehat D_{\rm within}^{U}=1-
 \frac{n_{-}(n_{-}-1)+n_{0}(n_{0}-1)+n_{+}(n_{+}-1)}{n(n-1)}.
 \label{eq:u_floor}
\end{equation}
The joint operational floor is intentionally conservative,
$D_{\rm joint\text{-}ID}=D_{\rm cross}-\max(D_{\rm joint,e_1},D_{\rm joint,e_2})$,
and is not assumed to add the measurement and model floors. Continuous rank
displacement is retained as a secondary magnitude diagnostic. Stable ordering
uses a tie-aware 15/15 seed cross-fit, a 95\% Beta interval and support of at
least eight strict observations; it does not select the headline metric.

\section{Biological context restructures which perturbations are reliable}
The Frangieh surface contains 243 shared perturbation labels across control,
co-culture and IFN$\gamma$. For every global five-fold label assignment, the
source predictor is fit once and its prediction vector is held fixed while the
target raw-cell outcome changes. We use delta cosine, Systema centroid accuracy
and absolute-effect rank agreement, with 30 independent seeds and matched
full-size raw-cell pseudo-replicates. The primary evidence is organized by
source context and by the biological contrast being transferred, rather than
by a source-to-single-target forest.

The question is therefore not whether one context has a better average score,
but whether the identity of reliable perturbations survives the shift. Figure
~\ref{fig:atlas} answers this at the atlas level by keeping source context,
contrast and metric as separate coordinates.

Figure~\ref{fig:atlas} shows why the estimand must retain both the source and
the left/right contrast. The three metrics do not share a single transport
surface: the largest measurement-identifiable excess is observed for delta
cosine, while the joint-floor stress test can remain negative for
absolute-effect rank. These signs are interpreted only with their declared
floors and intervals; a negative joint-floor result is not evidence that the
underlying ordering distributions are identical.
The atlas is consequently a map of where the estimand is identifiable, not a
claim that one metric or one biological contrast represents all transport.

\section{Measurement depth determines when transport becomes identifiable}
We next hold the perturbation universe fixed at the highest matched budget and
resample 10, 20, 40, 80 and 160 cells per environment--perturbation
pseudoreplicate, together with the matched full-depth endpoint. For each
source-by-contrast cell, we retain the signed, raw
$D_{\rm meas\text{-}ID}(n)$ trajectory rather than clipping negative values or
forcing a universal ratio. The first budget within ten percent of the full
value defines a resolution summary; the first budget with a positive 90\%
lower interval defines detectability. If no budget meets a rule, the threshold
remains unavailable. This makes the measurement boundary an explicit property
of the cell and metric, not a universal cutoff.

The question is whether increasing raw-cell depth separates biological
reordering from measurement variation. The hero trajectory and ridge views
answer it without converting signed estimates into a favorable ratio.

\section{Reliability reordering propagates into experimental regret}
At decision budgets $k/n\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20,0.50\}$, each source context selects the
lowest source risk and the target evaluates the selected set. We report target
top-$k$ retention and normalized regret. Individual directed transfers retain
their canonical 2,000-draw perturbation-label-by-measurement-seed intervals.
Figure~\ref{fig:decision} summarizes transfer heterogeneity descriptively and
keeps this decision layer separate from the global ordering estimand.

The decision question is whether an identifiable ordering loss changes what
would be selected under a finite budget. Figure~\ref{fig:decision} places that
link in the hero position and keeps budget and decomposition views subordinate.

The prespecified full-depth, 10\% link contains six directed biological
transfers and the three metrics. Its Spearman association is $\rho=0.80$;
metric-specific associations are not treated as headline tests because each
contains only six transfers. The panel is retained even if a future replication
weakens the association: the scientific question is whether identifiable
ordering loss has a measurable decision consequence, not whether a single
summary correlation is universal.
The association therefore supports decision relevance without establishing a
causal mechanism or a universal metric law.

\section{Transport failure is easier to measure than to predict prospectively}
The prospective analysis uses only source-side prediction geometry and model
uncertainty: $\texttt{uq\_cosine\_disagreement}$,
$\texttt{uq\_mean\_gene\_variance}$,
$\texttt{uq\_effect\_norm\_variance}$, $\texttt{prediction\_norm}$,
$\texttt{prediction\_sparsity}$ and $\texttt{prediction\_concentration}$.
Target outcomes, response displacement, effect magnitude, target risk and
target failure labels are excluded from the feature matrix. Leave-label-out
evaluation uses target outcomes only after predictions are made. The legal
within-dataset source-only results and the blocked Frangieh-to-Nadig
cross-dataset cell are shown separately.

The prospective question is stricter: what source-side information is legal
before target outcomes exist, and does it predict held-out transport reliably?
Figure~\ref{fig:prospective} makes that information boundary visible before
showing negative and near-chance task-level results.

\section{Discussion}
Three findings organize the study. First, source-frozen reliability orderings
change across matched biological contexts, and the change is metric-dependent
after measurement and model floors are made explicit. Second, measurement
depth determines which source--contrast cells become identifiable, so a visible
ordering shift is not automatically a transport claim. Third, once the shift is
identifiable, it propagates into top-$k$ retention and experimental regret, while
individual failures remain heterogeneous and only partly observed.

The central conclusion is: \emph{reliability transport failure becomes
experimentally identifiable and decision-relevant before it becomes
prospectively predictable.} The prospective analysis makes that asymmetry
operational. Source-only uncertainty and prediction geometry can be evaluated
before a target experiment, but negative or near-chance held-out performance is
itself part of the result rather than a reason to add target-informed features.

These findings support a limitation-centered interpretation. Failure anatomy is
restricted by matched legacy response fields; complete-row coverage is shown
explicitly rather than hidden by imputation. A negative joint-floor excess can
arise from conservative asymmetric floors and does not prove no biological
difference. It should trigger checks of measurement depth, model-member
disagreement, metric geometry and implementation identity before a mechanism is
assigned. The Nadig registry protects against choosing a favorable pair after
observing outcomes, but one replication cannot establish universal
transportability. The PTL source-selection gate uses a validation-only
acceptance criterion and remains a heuristic, while the outcome-blind Nadig
pair has supported independent-context provenance.
The PTL source-selection gate uses a validation-only acceptance criterion and
remains a heuristic; these decision rules do not turn descriptive transport
associations into causal claims.

Modern-model extensions remain outside the inferential claim:
GEARS has no frozen Frangieh/Nadig vector, TxPert is limited to its cached K562
contract, State has no promoted same-contract vector, and scGPT stops at the
Windows torchtext ABI. These are scope limits, not transport outcomes.
